Explorations

What’s that ablaut?

Ablaut is a change of the vowel inside a word to mark time: write, wrote, written. The consonants hold still and the vowel moves. It’s the oldest way English marks the past, older than -ed.

write, wrote, written

Two things at once: the vowel changing, and the spelling around it doing its usual jobs.

Say them in a frame: Today I write. Yesterday I wrote. I have written.

The box does it again

Long in the first two, short behind a box in the third.

Take the box away: the long o

One consonant, nothing to stop the vault: the e of -en reaches back, and the o says its name. This is the contrast that shows the box in written is doing the work.

Where the rule stops

Short i with one consonant and no box. In driven, striven and given, v can’t double (step 8), so there is no box to build. In risen, s could double and doesn’t. Each reads as a long i (“dryven”, “ryzen”) until a learner knows the word: the spelling shows the family, not the sound.

The box with a short o

The same box after a short o: the tt keeps the -en from reaching back.

i, a, u

The vowel steps through three short sounds and nothing else changes. The noun takes a fourth: song.

ow, ew, own

A team changes to another team, and -n joins the first one.

Back to the first vowel

The past changes the vowel, and the -en form goes back to the vowel of the present.

The long o and the short i

A pattern runs through these. When the past is spelled o-consonant-e, the -en form keeps the o and its vault: spoke, spoken; broke, broken; stole, stolen; woke, woken; wove, woven; froze, frozen; chose, chosen. The i verbs go the other way: drove, driven; rode, ridden; wrote, written; rose, risen; strove, striven. There the -en form goes back to an i, and the i is short, behind a box where the consonant can double and without one where it can’t or doesn’t.

Ablaut explains the vowel; the box explains the reading

The doubled t didn’t make the i of written short. The short vowel was there first, in the spoken word, and the spelling doubled the t to show it. So the box tells a reader how to say the word; ablaut tells why the vowel changed.

The -en takes a seam, as it does in been (be + en), driven and awaken. Some of these pairs are also on Spelled apart: knew and new, blew and blue. Every word here can be looked up in Words.
