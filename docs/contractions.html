Explorations

Squeezed into oblivion

In a contraction, two words join and the second one, small and quick, loses its vowel. The vowel is squeezed into oblivion, and an apostrophe lands where it was: an apostrophe catastrophe. The apostrophe is drawn red, in the vowel’s colour, with no circle: it holds the vowel’s place and says nothing.

not loses its o

The o of not is squeezed out and the apostrophe takes its place. In aren’t and weren’t the silent e of are and were stays: the first word keeps its whole spelling, and only not is squeezed.

The operator loses its vowel

After a pronoun, am, is, are and us lose their vowels. What’s left is a consonant, and sometimes a silent e, leaning on the word before it.

More than the vowel goes

Some lose a whole first part. One apostrophe stands for all of it.

Won’t keeps an old form of will, wol, so it changes more than the others. Don’t changes its vowel: do says the vowel of moon, don’t the name of o. Both are worth saying aloud beside the two words they come from.

Edward Carney, A Survey of English Spelling (1994): the apostrophe, p. 50. These are on the route at step 27, and the ones that sound like another word (it’s and its, you’re and your) are on Spelled apart. Every word here can be looked up in Words.
