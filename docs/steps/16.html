Affixes and roots

A weak vowel alone in its syllable, as around. Each is marked as it’s said on its own; inside a word many are said weakly.

New marks

none: a long first vowel has its long mark, and nothing sends it a vault; a short one takes the consonant into its syllable (sev·en); a weak one stays bare. Where a suffix took a dropped e's place, its vowel sends the vault: mus(e) + ic

The move

Try the first vowel long, then short, then weak. Keep the one that makes a word you know, then mark it.

None at this step.

Carney (1994)

In a vowel-consonant-vowel run the first vowel is often short, and hard to predict without stress, p. 275; open syllables, p. 134

Research

Kearns (2020), whose example word for this pattern is tiger: with one consonant between two vowels, the first vowel of a two-syllable word is long 47% of the time, short 25% and weak 21%. In longer words it is long only 19% of the time. The vowel matters: u is usually long (84%), and e is long only 18% of the time and weak 60%. The full references.
