Legomorphic spelling · One analog, several words

say, pay, lay

At the end of a word this sound is spelled ay. Inside a word it is spelled ai. Three verbs add a bare d for the past. The d closes the word, so ay turns to ai.

This list goes with Step 22: A team's second sound.

Print this list: the teacher’s notes are left off.

The words

I say yes.
say + s → saysShe says yes.
sa(y→i) + d → saidHe said no.
We pay here.
pa(y→i) + d → paidI paid for it.
Hens lay eggs.
la(y→i) + d → laidThe hen laid an egg.

Says and said are said with the e of bed. The spelling still shows say.

The contrast

Most verbs that end in ay keep the y and add ed.

For the teacher

Said, paid and laid are built the same way: the y becomes i and a bare d is added. That build is itself the exception. Other verbs in -ay keep the y and add -ed: played, stayed, prayed, delayed. Brooks calls laid and paid “irregularly-spelt (but not irregularly-pronounced)” (p. 196).

A reason for the i still shows. ay is the spelling at the end of a word, and ai never ends a word (Brooks, p. 191). Once d closes the word, the vowel takes its inside spelling. Daily, gaily, lain and slain do the same (Brooks, p. 230).

Said and says also shift their vowel. Do does the same in does and done. The spelling keeps the base and the sound moves, so these are words to spell by their family, not by their sound.

The check uses words that aren’t on the list. A learner who writes repaid and mislaid has the convention; one who writes prayed and delayed has its limit too.

Have the learner build each word from its sum, not copy it, and say each sentence before writing it. Six to eight words is the whole list: the convention is what’s being learned.
