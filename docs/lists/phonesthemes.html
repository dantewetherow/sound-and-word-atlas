Legomorphic spelling · Sound and meaning

Sound and meaning: phonesthemes

Some groups of words share both a sound and a meaning. Glow, gleam and glitter all start with gl- and all have to do with light. Sniff, snout and sneeze start with sn- and all have to do with the nose. A sound shared like this is a phonestheme.

It isn’t a morpheme. Take gl- away and -ow, -eam and -itter mean nothing, so there’s no word sum. And it isn’t a rule: every list here ends with words that have the letters and not the meaning. The word is J. R. Firth’s (1930), from Greek phōnē, sound, and aisthēma, what is felt.

To teach: mix the words up and have learners sort them by meaning first, then look at how they start or end. Include the words that don’t fit, so learners see that the pattern has exceptions. The word study walks a class through it.

gl-sn-sl-sw-tw-str--ash-ump-owlfl-

gl- light and seeing

Same letters, not the meaning

Most of these share an old root, *ghel-, to shine, with gold and yellow. So the gl- light words are partly one word family and partly words that joined the group later. One count of written English found that about 4 in 10 different gl- words had to do with light or seeing. So did 6 in 10 of their uses (Bergen 2004). gl- held up in a count of the whole dictionary.

sn- the nose and mouth

Same letters, not the meaning

Sneeze was fnese in Old English. The fn- dropped out of use and sn- took its place, perhaps because sn- suited the nose (Liberman 2019). Bergen (2004) tested sn- with gl-. sn- held up in a count of the whole dictionary.

sl- sliding and slipping

Same letters, not the meaning

Firth’s own first example (1930). The dictionary count didn’t back it up: plenty of sl- words don’t slide. Many readers notice the pattern; the numbers do not support it. sl- didn’t hold up in a count of the whole dictionary.

sw- swinging and sweeping

Same letters, not the meaning

Each of these moves like a pendulum or a broom: out and back, or round. sw- held up in a count of the whole dictionary.

tw- twisting

The family of two

The second row is a real family: all from the old word for two (Indo-European *dwo-). Twist and twine are in it too, as thread of two strands, and a twig is where a branch splits in two. Twirl, tweak and twitch share only the sound and a related meaning. tw- held up in a count of the whole dictionary.

str- long and thin

Same letters, not the meaning

Each names something long and narrow, or the act of making something longer. str- held up in a count of the whole dictionary.

-ash a hard, fast hit

Same letters, not the meaning

In wash, the w changes the sound of the a, so it does not belong to the group. A phonestheme is a shared sound; the same letters with a different sound do not count. -ash held up in a count of the whole dictionary.

-ump a rounded lump

Same letters, not the meaning

Each names a small, rounded mass or a raised shape. -ump held up in a count of the whole dictionary.

-owl angry sounds and looks, and stealthy movement

Same letters, not the meaning

Bowl has the letters but not the sound: its ow spells the o of snow, so it does not belong to the group. -owl held up in a count of the whole dictionary.

fl- quick, light movement

Same letters, not the meaning

Just short of significance in the dictionary count, so the evidence is weaker than for gl- or sn-. fl- came close to holding up in a count of the whole dictionary.

Sources

J. R. Firth, Speech (1930), who named the phonestheme; John Wallis noticed groups like these in his grammar of English (1653). Dwight Bolinger, “The sign is not arbitrary” (1949), and Hans Marchand, “Phonetic symbolism in English word-formation” (1959). Benjamin Bergen, “The psychological reality of phonaesthemes,” Language 80 (2004): 290–311: gl- and sn- words primed each other more than words that share only letters or only meaning. Katya Otis and Eyal Sagi, “Phonaesthemes: a corpus-based analysis” (Cognitive Science Society, 2008): which clusters hold up across the whole dictionary. Anatoly Liberman, “Sniff, snuff, SNAFU,” OUPblog (2019), on sneeze. The roots *ghel- and *dwo- are from the Indo-European Lexicon (University of Texas at Austin, after Pokorny).
