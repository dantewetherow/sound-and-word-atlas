Parts · a root family

*deuk-*deuk- means ‘lead’

To lead, to pull along. Latin made this Proto-Indo-European root into dūcere, to lead, and with a prefix in front it says which way: forth, back, in, out, aside, together. English borrowed the verbs with duce and their nouns with duct, and kept its own words from the same root, tow and tug. The family teaches two things: why reduce makes reduction, and how stress tells proDUCE from PROduce.

These words come from one old word that meant lead or pull.

  • produce
  • product
  • reduce
  • conduct
  • educate
  • duct
  • tug

duce: lead

Latin dūcere, to lead, in its present form, after a prefix that says which way.

The final e does two jobs at once: it sends the vault that makes the u long (step 9) and it nudges the c soft (step 10). Before a vowel suffix the e drops and the suffix’s vowel takes over both jobs: producer, reducible, conducive.

+ + → producerone who makes or brings out
+ + → reproducemake again; make a copy
+ → reducelead back: bring down, make smaller
+ + → reducibleable to be made smaller
+ → introducelead inside: bring in, make known
+ → inducelead into: bring about, persuade
+ → deducelead down from: work out by reasoning
+ → educelead out: draw out
+ → adducelead toward: bring forward as proof
+ → seducelead aside, lead astray
+ → conducelead together: help to bring about
+ + → conducivehelping to bring about
+ → traducelead across, as a show: speak ill of
+ + → transducera device that carries energy across from one form to another

duct: led

Latin ductus, led, the past form of dūcere.

Duce and duct take turns, as lig and lect do. The verb ends in duce; -ion, -ive and -or join Latin’s past form, duct: produce and production, reduce and reduction, induce and induction. A speller who hears duk-shun can write duct + ion: the c is hard because a t follows it, and ti spells the sh.

duc: bring up

Latin ēducāre, to bring up, to rear, a verb that stands beside ēdūcere, to lead out.

Educate and educe are near twins: e + duc + ate and e + duce. To educate was first to rear a child; the sense of leading out what is in the learner is a later reading of the word, and a fair one.

+ + → educatebring up, teach
+ + + → educationbringing up, teaching
+ + + → educatorone who teaches
+ + + + → educationalhaving to do with teaching

duke, duch, duit, due: a leader; and words worn down in French

Latin dux, a leader, became French duc; and Latin conductus and subdūcere lost letters on their way through French.

→ dukea leader; a noble next below a prince
+ → duchessa woman of a duke’s rank
duchythe land a duke rules
+ → ducalhaving to do with a duke
+ → conduita channel that leads water or wires: conduct, through French
+ → subduebring under control; its sense was shaped by another Latin verb, subdere, to put under

tow, tug, tie, team: pull: English’s own words

Old English togian, to pull, and its kin, which English inherited and never borrowed. In the Germanic languages the root’s d became t, so these words start with t where Latin’s start with d.

Tug and duct are cousins: one came down through Old English, the other was borrowed from Latin. The same change of d to t sets two beside dual and ten beside decimal.

towpull along behind
tugpull hard
tiea cord that draws tight; to fasten with one
teamanimals pulling together in harness; now any group that works together

Stress tells the verb from the noun

Produce is two words with one spelling. The verb is proDUCE: farms produce food. The noun is PROduce: the food itself. Conduct works the same way: to conDUCT a band, but good CONduct. The verb keeps the stress where Latin had it, on the root. The noun pulls it to the front.

The marks show this only through the first vowel. In the verb the first syllable is said weakly, so its o stays bare. In the noun it is said in full, so the o takes a long mark. Nothing sends that o a vault: stress makes it long, and stress isn’t a letter. The notation has no mark for stress itself, so the captions use capitals for it, and the weak vowel is the only sign among the marks.

The pattern runs well beyond this family: record, permit, object, present, contract, rebel. A learner who knows it can hear which word is meant, and a speller can see why two words that sound different are written alike: the spelling keeps the parts, pro + duce, and leaves the stress to the speaker.

An ending can move the stress

Some suffixes decide where the stress falls. Before -ion it lands on the syllable just ahead of the suffix: EDucate becomes eduCAtion, and proDUCE becomes proDUCtion. -ity does the same: proDUCtive becomes producTIVity. When the stress moves, vowels change with it: the long a of educAtion is weak in EDucate’s cousin educator, and the spelling stays the same throughout.

This is where spelling by parts earns its keep. The sounds shift from word to word, and the parts don’t: e + duc + ate, e + duc + at(e) + ion, e + duc + at(e) + or.
