Parts · a root family

*leuk-*leuk- means ‘light’

Light, brightness: to shine. Latin made this Proto-Indo-European root into lūx, light, and lūcēre, to shine; into lūmen, a light; into lūstrāre, to make bright; and into lūna, the moon, the bright one. Greek made it leukos, white. English kept light. The family is the home of illustrate: to throw light on a thing, by an example or a picture.

These words come from one old word that meant light.

lustr, lustre: make bright; a shine

Latin lūstrāre, to make bright, to light up, joined in illūstrāre to in-, in, on.

To illustrate is to throw light on a thing. It meant that before it meant pictures: first to make bright or famous, then to make clear by examples, and only later to add pictures to a book.

+ + → illustratethrow light on: make clear, by an example or a picture
+ + + → illustrationan example or a picture that makes something clear
+ + + → illustrativeserving to make clear
+ + → illustriousshining: famous for what one has done
lustrea soft shine; luster in American spelling
+ → lacklustrewithout shine: dull

lumen, lumin: a light

Latin lūmen, a light; lūminis, of a light.

Latin wrote lumen on its own and lumin- with an ending after it, as English does: lumen, but lumin + ous.

+ + → illuminatelight up; make clear
+ + + → illuminationa lighting up
+ → luminousgiving off light
+ → luminarya body that gives light; a person who lights the way
→ lumena measure of light: what a bulb’s box counts

luc, lux: light; shine

Latin lūx, light, lūcis, of light, and lūcēre, to shine.

Lux is luc + s. The x spells the c and the s together, so the c is hidden. Take the s away and the c comes back: lucid, lucent.

+ → lucidclear: easy to understand
+ + + → elucidatemake clear: bring out into the light
+ → lucentshining
+ + → translucentletting light through, but not clear like glass
+ + → pellucidclear right through
→ luxa measure of the light falling on a surface

lun: the moon

Latin lūna, the moon, from an older *leuksnā: the bright one.

+ → lunarhaving to do with the moon

leuk: white, bright

Greek leukos, white, bright.

+ → leukemiaa cancer of the blood, named for its white cells; leukaemia in British spelling
+ → leukocytea white blood cell

light: light: English’s own word

Old English lēoht, light.

Not every light is this root’s. See Two lights.

+ → lightenmake brighter

Marginal or debated

Some words are often listed with the root, but the sources hedge the road back to it. They keep their word sums here, since the parts hold whatever the history. What’s in doubt is only where the root came from.

lynx: a wild cat

Greek lynx, through Latin.

The Indo-European Lexicon lists lynx with the root. Etymonline leaves it off its list for the root, and its entry for the word gives the link only as probable: the cat named for its sharp sight.

lynxa wild cat with sharp eyes

Il-: in- before l

Why two l’s in illustrate? The first belongs to the prefix. Latin in- became il- before the l of lustr and lumin, as it does before legal. Each part brings its own l: il + lustr + ate, il + lumin + ate.

Here in- means in, into: to put light into a thing. In illegal it means not. They are two Latin prefixes spelled alike, and both are written il- before l. Pellucid does the same with per-, through: pel + luc + id.

More on prefixes that change to match is with assimilate.

The c inside lux

Lux, light, ends in x, and x spells two sounds, /k/ and /s/. In Latin those were the c of the root and an ending s: luc + s. Wherever the s isn’t there, the c shows again: lucid, lucent, translucent, elucidate. A speller who knows the root is luc knows why those words have a c and not a k or an s.

Two lights

English has two words spelled light. One is this root’s: brightness, a lamp, to light a fire. The other means not heavy, and it comes from another root, *legwh-, which also gave lung, the light organ. They met in the same spelling by chance.

So lighten is two words as well: to make brighter, and to make less heavy. The sentence says which. The word sum is the same, light + en, because each light is a base in its own right.
