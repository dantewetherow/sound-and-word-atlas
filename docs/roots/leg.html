Parts · a root family

*leg-*leg- means ‘gather’

To gather, to collect. Speakers of Greek and Latin carried this Proto-Indo-European root into words about words: gathering words is choosing them, reading them and saying them. English borrowed the family back again and again, and the notation’s own name is one of them. How the poets used legō, in Latin and in Greek.

Word study: Gather, read, say a presentation for learners, built a piece at a time

The o is a part of its own, meaning “I,” so it spells its name, as in go.

lego: I gather

Greek légō and Latin legō, the verb itself, which both languages inherited from the root. In Greek, I gather, I count, I say; in Latin, I gather, I choose, I read.

The ō is the ending for I in both languages. So lego is leg + o, gather + I, as Latin veto is I forbid and credo I believe.

Marginal or debated

Some words are often listed with the root, but the sources call the link uncertain. They keep their word sums here, since the parts are the same whatever the history. Only the link to the root is in doubt.

leg, legis, loy, lege: law

Latin lēx, law; lēgis, of law, as in lēgis lātor, a proposer of law.

Watkins gives this branch as possible only, with a question mark on law as a ‘collection of rules’. He also names a possible but uncertain link to another root, *legh-, to lie, to lay. On that reading, law is what is laid down, the source of English law. Etymonline gives lēx only as perhaps from *leg-.

Legō in Latin and in Greek

Latin and Greek share the form legō, I gather, and each gave it a different meaning. In Latin, gathering turned to picking out, then to choosing and reading; in Greek, to counting, telling over and saying. So in legomorphic the Greek says ‘I gather forms’, and an English reader who knows Latin hears ‘I read forms’ too.

The Latin column follows the nine senses of legō in the Oxford Latin Dictionary (1968), from the hand to the eye. Each has a line where a poet used it. The two languages meet at the funeral pyre: Achilles and Tibullus use the same verb for gathering the bones of the dead. The translations are ours.

The weak point: a dictionary orders senses by meaning, not by date. Reading is already in Plautus, among the oldest Latin we have, and saying is already in Homer. So the change from gathering to reading and to saying happened before our earliest texts. The order here is a reconstruction, not something the texts let us watch happen.

Latin legō: I gather, I choose, I read

legō, legere, lēgī, lectum. The lect of collect and lecture is the last of these.

  1. with the hand: to pick, to plucklegit flōrēsshe gathers flowersOvid, Metamorphoses 4.315
  2. with the mouthē foliīs nātōs, ē suāvibus herbīs / ōre leguntthe bees gather their young from the leaves and the sweet grasses with their mouthsVirgil, Georgics 4.200–201. Virgil had the bees wrong, but the verb is right
  3. the last breathextrēmus sī quis super hālitus errat, / ōre legamif any last breath still wanders over you, I will gather it with my mouthVirgil, Aeneid 4.684–685: Anna to her sister Dido, dyingspīritus fugiēns meō legātur ōrelet your fleeing breath be gathered by my mouthSeneca, Hercules Oetaeus 1342: Alcmena to her son Hercules

    Commentators call it a last kindness, like closing the eyes of the dying.

  4. the bones, after the firequae legat in maestōs ossa perusta sinūsa mother to gather my burnt bones into her grieving lapTibullus 1.3.6, sick and far from home, with no mother there to do itextrēmō quī legat ossa diēsomeone to gather my bones on the last dayPropertius 2.24.50
  5. in a line: sails, ropes, threadnauita … ūmida vēla legitthe sailor gathers in his wet sailsVirgil, Georgics 1.373extrēmaque Lausō / Parcae fīla leguntand the Fates gather in the last threads for LaususVirgil, Aeneid 10.814–815
  6. a path: to follow tracks, to trace a shorevestīgia retrō / observāta legithe picks up his own footprints, going back the way he cameVirgil, Aeneid 9.392–393: Nisus, looking for his friendcrebrīs legimus freta consita terrīswe thread the straits, sown thick with islandsVirgil, Aeneid 3.127
  7. with the eyes and the ears: to pick outunde omnīs longō ōrdine posset / adversōs legerefrom where he could pick them all out, face to face, in their long lineVirgil, Aeneid 6.754–755: Anchises, watching the souls go byunde hōrum sermōnem legamwhere I can pick up what they sayPlautus, Pseudolus 414: listening in

  9. to readnōmen … in marmore lectum / perfūdit lacrimīsshe drenched with her tears the name she read on the marbleOvid, Metamorphoses 2.338: Clymene at her son Phaethon’s tombin tōtō plūrimus orbe legorall over the world, I am read more than anyoneOvid, Tristia 4.10.128, from exile

Greek legō: I gather, I count, I say

λέγω. Its noun is λόγος, logos: a gathering of words, an account, a reason.

  1. to gatherὀστέα Πατρόκλοιο Μενοιτιάδαο λέγωμενlet us gather the bones of Patroclus, son of MenoetiusHomer, Iliad 23.239: Achillesὀστέα λευκὰ λέγοντοthey gathered the white bonesHomer, Iliad 24.793: Hector’s brothers and friends
  2. to count: to gather one by oneλέκτο δ’ ἀριθμόνhe counted their numberHomer, Odyssey 4.451: Proteus, counting his sealsἐν δ’ ἡμέας πρώτους λέγε κήτεσινhe counted us first among the beastsOdyssey 4.452: Menelaus and his men, hidden in sealskins
  3. to tell over: to lay out a story piece by pieceἴσκε ψεύδεα πολλὰ λέγων ἐτύμοισιν ὁμοῖαhe told many lies, and made them like the truthHomer, Odyssey 19.203: Odysseus, in disguise, to his wife
  4. to sayIn later Greek the everyday word for speaking, and the root of lexicon, logic, dialogue and prologue

One more line in the Odyssey looks the same and isn’t: ἔπειτα δὲ λέκτο καὶ αὐτός, “then he lay down himself” (4.453). That lekto comes from another verb, to lie down, kin to λέχος, a bed. Two lines apart, one form counts the seals and the other goes to sleep among them.
