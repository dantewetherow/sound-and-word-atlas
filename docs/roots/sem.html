Parts · a root family

*sem-*sem- means ‘one’ or ‘same’

One; as one, together. From this Proto-Indo-European root Latin made similis, like, and simul, at the same time; Greek made homos, same; and English kept same and some. The family carries a lesson in its own spelling. Assimilate means to make like, and its double s is a prefix made like its neighbour.

These words come from one old word that meant one or same.

  • some

  • simple

simil: like

Latin similis, like, of the same kind.

Assimilate does what it says. It is ad + simil + ate, to make like, and the d of ad- was itself made like the s that follows: as + simil + ate. That is why it has two s’s.

+ → similaralike
+ + → similaritya likeness
+ + → dissimilarnot alike
similea likeness drawn in words: as brave as a lion
+ + → assimilatemake like; take in and make part of oneself
+ + + → assimilationa making like: in speech, one sound made like its neighbour

simul: together, at one time; make like

Latin simul, at the same time, and simulāre, to make like, to copy.

+ → simulatecopy, act like
+ + → simulationa copy that acts like the real thing
simultaneoushappening at one time

semble: together; like: worn down in French

Latin simul and similis, through French, where the vowels changed and a b grew between the m and the l.

Assemble has the same front double as assimilate, for the same reason: ad + semble, written as + semble.

+ → assemblebring together
assemblya gathering; a putting together
+ → resemblebe like
+ + → resemblancea likeness
+ → ensembleall the parts together; a group that plays as one

sim, sin: one

Latin simplex, one-fold, and singulus, one at a time.

+ → simpleof one fold: not mixed, not hard
+ + → simplicitybeing simple
singleone only
singularone of a kind; in grammar, naming one

homo: same

Greek homos, same.

Three words a teacher of spelling uses. To, two and too are homophones; produce the verb and produce the noun are homographs.

+ → homophonea word with the same sound as another
+ → homographa word written the same as another
+ → homonyma word with the same name as another: the same sound, the same spelling, or both

A double at the front of a word

A double near the end of a word usually marks a suffix joining a short vowel: get + t + ing (step 14). A double near the front usually marks something else: a Latin prefix whose last letter changed to match the first letter of the root. Two different consonants in a row take two movements of the mouth; one held a little longer takes one. Speakers took the easier path, and the spelling recorded it.

ad-, to, toward: accept, affix, allot, appear, assemble, attract. com-, with: collect, correct, conduct. in-, not, or into: illegal, immature, irregular. sub-, under: succeed, suffix, support. ob-, against: occur, offer, oppose. dis-, apart: differ. ex-, out: effect.

Affix and suffix are themselves examples: ad + fix, fastened to, and sub + fix, fastened under. The words for word parts were built the way they describe.

Accommodate: two doubles, two joins

Accommodate is ad + com + mod + ate: to fit one thing to another. The d of ad- became c before the c of com-, which gives the two c’s. Then com- met the m of mod, a measure, which gives the two m’s with no change at all. A speller who knows the parts writes ac + com + mod + ate and has nothing to remember. Its family is *med-, to measure.

A test for the speller

Take the front off and see what is left. Collect without col leaves lect, which turns up in select and elect: a prefix and a root, so the double belongs. Assemble without as leaves semble, as in resemble.

Not every front double is a prefix that changed. In unnatural, misspell and dissatisfied nothing changed: each part brought its own letter, un + natural, mis + spell, dis + satisfied. And some doubles have no join at all: rabbit, lesson. The family is what shows a join, as it does for happy.
