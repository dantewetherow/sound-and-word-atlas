Derivations · a base family

haphap means ‘luck’

Chance, luck. English took hap from Old Norse happ around 1200. It’s rarely said on its own now, but it lives on in happy, happen and mishap, and the Indo-European Lexicon traces it back to a root *kob-, to fit, to fare well. Its spelling shows how it joins: happ before a vowel, hap everywhere else.

These words come from hap. It is an old word for luck.

  • happy
  • happen
  • mishap
  • perhaps

One base, two spellings

The p doubles only when a vowel comes next, as the n of fun does in funny, so the box can keep the a short (step 14). Before a consonant nothing reaches back, and English doesn’t end a word in pp (step 6), so one p does the job.

The family

Each word with its parts and what it means, grouped by what comes after hap. Each word links to its history in Etymonline.

Before a vowel

happ: the p doubles and the a stays short. The n of happening stays single, because en isn’t stressed, as in opening.

  • happyhapp + yhaving hap: lucky, at first, then glad
  • happenhapp + ento come about, at first by chance
  • happilyhapp + i + lyin a happy way
  • happinesshapp + i + nessbeing happy
  • unhappyun + happ + ynot happy
  • happeninghapp + en + ingsomething that happens

Before a consonant

hap: nothing reaches back to the a, so one p is enough.

  • haplesshap + lesswithout luck
  • haplyhap + lyby chance: an old word for perhaps
  • haphazardhap + hazardby chance, with no plan

At the end

hap: English doesn’t end a word in pp.

  • mishapmis + hapbad luck, a small accident
  • perhapsper + hap + sby chances: maybe
  • mayhapmay + hapit may happen: an old word for perhaps

Letter pairs the seam splits

Read these without the seam and they go wrong. In mishap the s and the h meet across the seam, so they don’t spell sh: mis + hap, not mish + ap. In haphazard the p and the h meet the same way, so they don’t spell the sound of ph in phone: hap + hazard. Shepherd (sheep + herd) and uphill work like this too.

What happy meant

Happy is hap + y: having hap. Its first meaning, in the late 1300s, was lucky, favoured by fortune, and the sense of glad came in the same century. Lucky, luck + y, is built the same way, and means now what happy meant then. The y is the ending of sunny and funny: having, or full of, what the base names. Read without hap, happy means being in a happy state, which goes in a circle. With hap, it means having luck.

Does the double p show the seam?

Not on its own. A double keeps a short vowel short whether or not there’s a join: rabbit and lesson double with nothing to join. Carney counts happy among the ordinary doubles, with hobby, silly and merry, and says the hidden boundary in chubby and shabby needn’t be known to explain theirs. What shows the seam is the family: the same hap, with the same meaning, in hapless and mishap, where nothing doubles. Fun is free, a word on its own, so the seam in funny is easy to see; hap is bound now, so the family does the showing.

Nor does the double p spell two sounds. Carney lists pp among the spellings of the single sound /p/ (p. 16). The lips close once, at the end of ha, and open once, into the y, and the break between the syllables falls inside that one sound, so it seems to belong to both. Daniel Kahn called such a consonant ambisyllabic; Elisabeth Selkirk put it in the first syllable, as the swoops here do. The puff of breath in pen isn’t a second p: English adds it where a p starts a stressed syllable, so appear has it and happy doesn’t. Two p sounds meet only where two parts each bring one, as in top pick, and the lips stay shut longer; joins inside words, such as unnamed, work the same way.
