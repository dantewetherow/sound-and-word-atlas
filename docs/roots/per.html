Parts · a root family: a word study

*per-*per- means ‘try’ or ‘risk’

To try, to risk. From this Proto-Indo-European root Latin made experīrī, to try out, with expertus, tried, and perīculum, a trial or a danger. Greek made peira, a trial, and English kept fear. At the centre of the family are two words that began as one, experiment and experience. They have the same spelling up to the i and two different sounds for the e; the history explains both.

Word study: Try it out a presentation for learners, built a piece at a time

These words come from one old word that meant try, or take a risk.

  • experiment
  • experience
  • expert
  • peril
  • pirate
  • fear

Say them slowly: ex-per-i-ment, ex-pe-ri-ence.

In per, the r closes the syllable, so the e is short.

In pe, the syllable is open, so the e spells its name.

Both words have an i after per. It came with the old Latin word, and it’s in both. You don’t need to know why it’s there to read them.

A pirate was someone who tried an attack. Fear is what you feel when danger is near. Same old word.

How the words came into English

The root came into English four ways, each with its own spellings. This is the time-lapse, or diachronic, view. Pick a spelling to see its words.

Two words that were one

Latin had one verb, experīrī, to try out, and made nouns from it that English took twice. Here is the time-lapse, the diachronic view:

  1. Proto-Indo-European *per-to try, to risk
  2. Latin experīrī, to try outOne verb, three words from it. Experīmentum, a trial or a proof; experientia, a trying, and the knowing that comes of it; expertus, tried
  3. French, then English, in the 1300sExperiment and experience arrive meaning nearly the same thing: a trial, a proof, what trying shows. The Middle English Dictionary’s experiment is a test, a proof, even a tried remedy for an illness
  4. English sinceThe two drift apart. An experiment becomes the test you set up; experience, what you have lived through
  5. French todayStill one word. Expérience means both, and a French science class does une expérience

Short e, long e: the letter after the i

Say them slowly: ex-per-i-ment, ex-pe-ri-ence. The letters match up to the i, but the e is short in one and spells its name in the other. The letter that decides it is the one after the i. In experience the i stands before a vowel, the e of -ence, and makes the e long across the r. The notation draws that vault. In experiment the i stands before the m of -ment, and nothing reaches back. Where the syllables break, ex-per-i-ment and ex-pe-ri-ence, follows from the sound, so it describes the difference rather than causing it. That is the snapshot, the synchronic view: what English does now.

Underneath are two patterns that Chomsky and Halle set out in The Sound Pattern of English. A stressed vowel with two syllables after it tends to be short: divine but divinity, serene but serenity, nation but national. That is experiment. But a vowel before one consonant, then an i, then another vowel is usually long, even with two syllables after it. Canada but Canadian, comedy but comedian, colony but colonial. That is experience, where the i stands before the e of -ence.

They are tendencies, not laws. Obesity keeps the long e of obese, and Italian has a short a where Canadian has a long one. Teach the i before a vowel, which the vault shows, and let the family carry the rest.

Expect speech to vary here, too. Before an r is where it varies most: Merriam-Webster gives ik-SPER-i-ment, also ik-SPIR-i-ment. A learner who says the second has the same parts and the same spelling; mark what the learner says.

A note on the i, for the curious

Both words have an i after per, and it is the same i. It is the stem vowel of Latin’s verb, experīrī, whose stem ends in ī as audīre’s does. Latin built both nouns on that stem: experī + mentum, experī + ent + ia. Expert shows the root without it. It comes from the past form expertus, built on exper- alone. So the root is per, and the i belongs to the verb’s stem.

It has no meaning of its own. It marks which class of verb Latin’s word belonged to, as the a of ēducāre and the e of docēre do. A linguist calls it a theme vowel. It isn’t a connecting vowel either, like the i of carnivore or the o of photograph, which join two parts and belong to neither. In English its only job is in the sound. Before the e of -ence it makes the e of pe long; before the m of -ment it doesn’t.

None of this is needed to teach the family. The word sums on this page write the stem as one part, peri, because every word in that cluster keeps the i. A learner who knows ex + peri + ment and ex + peri + ence can read, spell and connect them. The i is a deeper point, here for anyone who wants it.

The stress walks, and the spelling holds

Add a suffix and the stress moves. Every vowel it leaves goes weak, and a weak vowel it lands on comes back full. The letters hold still while the sounds change, and that is why the spelling is a better guide to the family than the sound.

Experiential has a t where experience has a c: -ence and -ential, as in essence and essential, confidence and confidential. Latin wrote both with a t, experientia. French and English wrote the noun with a c and kept the t in -ential. There the t spells sh before -ial, as it does in notation.

Large and red: the main stress. Middle: a lighter stress. Small and grey: weak, where the vowel is said quickly, often as the sound in the a of about.

One spelling, three sounds

In the Latin words the root keeps one spelling, per, and its e spells three sounds. Per is closed and short in experiment and peril, open and long in experience, and an r unit in expert, as in her. The Greek words do the same with their i: open and long in pirate, closed and short in empirical.

For the speller

Per, not pir or pur. The vowel is weak in experiment and hard to hear. Expert and peril say the e plainly, and they’re the same root.

-iment, not -ament. The i is the stem’s own vowel, and the same i is in experience: ex + peri + ment, ex + peri + ence.

-ience, not -iance. The noun comes from Latin’s present form, experient-, with an e, as patience goes with patient. And where the stress lands on it, the t comes back: experiential.
