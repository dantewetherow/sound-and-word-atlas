Parts · a root family

*med-*med- means ‘measure’

To take the right measure. Latin made this Proto-Indo-European root into modus, a measure or a manner, and into verbs for healing and thinking over, which are measures of another kind. English kept mete and must from the same root. The family is the home of a word people find hard to spell, accommodate, which is easy once its parts are seen.

These words come from one old word that meant measure.

  • mode
  • model
  • modern
  • medicine
  • remedy
  • must
  • accommodate

mod, after com-: with due measure: fitting

Latin commodus, com + modus, with measure: fitting, convenient.

Com + mod doubles the m with nothing changed: each part brings its own. Put ad- in front and its d turns to c before the c of com: ac + com + mod + ate.

mete, must: measure; be allowed: English’s own words

Old English metan, to measure, and mōtan, to be allowed, to have room to.

Must is on the route as an operator. It began as the past form of a verb for being allowed, for having the measure of time or room to do a thing.

Other roots

Accommodate: two doubles, two joins

Accommodate is ad + com + mod + ate: to fit one thing to the measure of another. It has two doubles, and they aren’t the same kind.

The two c’s are a prefix that changed. The d of ad- became c before the c of com-, as it does in accept and accuse. The two m’s are two parts meeting: com- ends in m and mod begins with one, and nothing changed. A speller who writes the parts, ac + com + mod + ate, has no letters to count.

More on prefixes that change to match is with assimilate, whose own spelling shows it.
