What it practises. The small words that carry most sentences, pronouns and operators, read with high-frequency actions and nouns in a sentence, not a list. Every word is marked, and the first word takes a capital.

A routine. Build a claim and read it. Flip it and read the question. Swap one word and read again. Then ask a learner to build one that’s true about them. Silly sentences are welcome: A fish could ride. They show a learner is reading, not guessing.

The grammar. An actor, an operator and an action make a claim. A question in English puts the operator in front of the actor. Because the operator carries the tense and the agreement, the action stays bare: she could go, never she could goes. That is why every combination here is a sentence. A claim with no operator (She goes.) borrows do to ask: Does she go? The one agreement to watch is do and does: does goes with one actor that isn’t I or you.

Prepositions. A preposition places the action: She could go in there. She could go over here. Most want something after them, a place or a thing: in there, under the box. A few can stand alone after the action, where they change what it means: go up, get out, sit down. Home needs no preposition: go home, not go to home. Let learners find that one by ear.

Not and n’t. Not stays behind the actor when the sentence flips: Could she not go? Squeezed to n’t, it joins the operator and travels with it: Couldn’t she go? May, might and must stop at not here: mayn’t and mightn’t are rare now. The squeeze is on Squeezed into oblivion.

From a claim to a question

An actor, an operator and an action make a claim. When the operator moves to the front, the claim becomes a question. The Dialogue Team acts it out with their faces: their words with the letters lifted out.

Cards for building sentences

From the first session of the staff series, on Presentations. Print them in colour on card stock.

  • Operator cardsFold-over tent cards for the nine modals (can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might and must), marked on one face and blank on the other, plus three blank cards.
  • Using the operator cardsMaking the cards, running a loop, and marking a word by hand.
  • Sentence cardsA demonstration set of 48 tent cards about reading and writing. Stand up an actor, an operator and an action, then move the operator to the front to turn the statement into a question.
  • Verb-system cardsAdded after the session: 53 tent cards for the words that orbit the verb (every form of be, have and do, the modals, not and the n’t contractions, the semi-modals, the endings and to). The first page says which words can move to the front to make a question and gives flips to try.

  • Actor cards107 tent cards for actors and the acted-on: twelve personal pronouns and Dolch’s 95 nouns. The first page lists them and gives flips to try.
  • Modal cards, first versionThe first version of the operator cards, from 8 October: the nine modals as fold-over tent cards, plus three blank cards. It boxes a doubled letter, like the ll of will, where the operator cards put a bar under it.
