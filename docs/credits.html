Credits

Credits and copyright

Legomorphic Notation stands on other people’s work. These are the writers it draws on, and the terms for using what’s here.

Spelling and words

  • Edward Carney, A Survey of English Spelling (Routledge, 1994). The backbone of the route, cited by page on every step.
  • Richard Venezky, “English Orthography: Its Graphical Structure and Its Relation to Sound,” Reading Research Quarterly 2 (1967), and The Structure of English Orthography (Mouton, 1970). Spelling as a system, including the letters that mark how other letters are read, such as a silent e: the job the vault, the nudge and the cone show. His later book is The American Way of Spelling (Guilford, 1999).
  • Greg Brooks, Dictionary of the British English Spelling System (Open Book Publishers, 2015), open access (doi). Every spelling of every sound, with its frequency and its exceptions. A doubled letter spells one sound, and happy is among his doubled p’s, on the hap page.
  • Noam Chomsky and Morris Halle, The Sound Pattern of English (Harper & Row, 1968). Their argument that English spelling keeps a word’s parts the same when their sounds change, as in sign and signature, is the premise word sums rest on.
  • Carol Chomsky, “Reading, Writing, and Phonology,” Harvard Educational Review 40, no. 2 (1970): 287–309 (doi). She carried that morphophonemic view of spelling into teaching learners to read and write.
  • Melvyn Ramsden, whose Real Spelling gave us word sums and word matrices, the basis of the grade 3 word sum cards. His book is Rescuing Spelling (Southgate Publishers).
  • Pete Bowers, who brought word sums and matrices into classrooms as structured word inquiry. His book is Teaching How the Written Word Works. With John R. Kirby and S. Hélène Deacon he wrote “The Effects of Morphological Instruction on Literacy Skills: A Systematic Review of the Literature,” Review of Educational Research 80, no. 2 (2010) (doi), and with Jeffrey Bowers, “Beyond Phonics: The Case for Teaching Children the Logic of the English Spelling System,” Educational Psychologist 52, no. 2 (2017): 124–141 (doi).
  • Sue Scibetta Hegland, Beneath the Surface of Words: What English Spelling Reveals and Why It Matters (Learning About Spelling).
  • E. W. Dolch, “A Basic Sight Vocabulary,” The Elementary School Journal 36, no. 6 (1936): 456–460. The Dolch cards and the Word lists page.
  • Edward Fry, whose first 100 Instant Words hold the three words on the Early Literacy Profile (Central Okanagan) lists that aren’t Dolch’s: each, other and people.
  • Central Okanagan Public Schools, Early Literacy Profile (ELP) Assessment & Instructional Guide (2025), pp. 16–17, for the ELP word lists. The cards made from them aren’t an SD23 publication.

Words and their histories

  • Douglas Harper, Online Etymology Dictionary. The histories behind Parts: every word in a family links to its entry.
  • Calvert Watkins, The American Heritage Dictionary of Indo-European Roots, 3rd ed. (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2011). The roots are to follow it; the families aren’t checked against it yet.
  • The Linguistics Research Center, University of Texas at Austin, Indo-European Lexicon, which follows Julius Pokorny, Indogermanisches etymologisches Wörterbuch (1959). The English, Latin and Greek words from each root, as on the *leg- and hap pages.
  • Wiktionary, for the verb in both languages, Greek λέγω and Latin legō, and for the root, *leǵ-.
  • Ferdinand de Saussure, Course in General Linguistics (1916), for synchronic and diachronic.
  • Leonard Bloomfield, Language (1933), for free and bound forms.

Sounds and syllables

  • Daniel Kahn, Syllable-Based Generalizations in English Phonology (PhD diss., MIT, 1976), and Elisabeth Selkirk, “The Syllable,” in The Structure of Phonological Representations, Part II, ed. Harry van der Hulst and Norval Smith (Foris, 1982). Kahn put a consonant like the p of happy in both syllables; Selkirk put it in the first, as the swoops do. Alice Turk measured lip movements to test the two: “Articulatory Phonetic Clues to Syllable Affiliation,” in Phonological Structure and Phonetic Form, ed. Patricia Keating (Cambridge University Press, 1994), 107–135 (publisher).
  • Catherine Anderson and colleagues, Essentials of Linguistics, an open textbook: “Aspirated Stops in English,” on the puff of breath after a p, t or k that starts a stressed syllable.
  • Sonia Ben Hedia, Gemination and Degemination in English Affixation (Language Science Press, 2019), open access (publisher). English has two of the same consonant sound in a row only across a join, as in unnamed.

Reading and learning

  • Daniil Elkonin. Sound circles go back to his boxes, where a learner pushes a counter into a box for each sound.
  • Linnea Ehri, “Orthographic Mapping in the Acquisition of Sight Word Reading, Spelling Memory, and Vocabulary Learning,” Scientific Studies of Reading 18, no. 1 (2014): 5–21 (doi). Her account of how readers bond a word’s spelling to its sounds and meaning is why it helps to mark which letters spell which sound.
  • Norbert Wiener, Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine (1948), for the idea of feedback the first session is built on.
  • The studies behind marking by hand are cited on Using the operator cards, in the Session 1 handouts.

Research on teaching the spelling system

Studies that bear on whether teaching English spelling as a system helps learners read and spell, kept here so the site’s claims can be checked against them. Bowers, Kirby and Deacon’s review is under Pete Bowers, above.

  • Kathleen Rastle, Clare Lally, Matthew H. Davis and J. S. H. Taylor, “The Dramatic Impact of Explicit Instruction on Learning to Read in a New Writing System,” Psychological Science 32, no. 4 (2021) (doi). Adults learning an invented writing system read new words far better after a short lesson on how its spellings map to sounds and meanings than after reading alone.
  • Steve Graham and Tanya Santangelo, “Does Spelling Instruction Make Students Better Spellers, Readers, and Writers? A Meta-Analytic Review,” Reading and Writing 27 (2014): 1703–1743 (record). Formal spelling teaching improved spelling, the gains lasted and carried into writing, and reading improved too.
  • V. Devonshire, P. Morris and M. Fluck, “Spelling and Reading Development: The Effect of Teaching Children Multiple Levels of Representation in Their Orthography,” Learning and Instruction 25 (2013): 85–94 (record). Five to seven year olds taught morphology, etymology and spelling rules read and spelled better than those taught phonics alone.
  • Danielle Colenbrander, Alexa von Hagen, Sascha Kohnen and colleagues, “The Effects of Morphological Instruction on Literacy Outcomes for Children in English-Speaking Countries: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis,” Educational Psychology Review 36 (2024): 119 (doi). Across 28 studies, small effects on reading and spelling overall, with low certainty; larger effects on the words that were taught; some transfer to spelling untaught words, none to reading them.
  • Danielle Colenbrander, Lindsay Parsons, Jeffrey S. Bowers and Colin J. Davis, “Assessing the Effectiveness of Structured Word Inquiry for Students in Grades 3 and 5 with Reading and Spelling Difficulties: A Randomized Controlled Trial,” Reading Research Quarterly 57, no. 1 (2022): 307–352 (doi). Structured word inquiry didn’t clearly beat an active comparison, apart from spelling untaught words in grade 3, and the teaching assistants found it harder to deliver.
  • Devin M. Kearns, “Does English Have Useful Syllable Division Patterns?” Reading Research Quarterly 55, no. S1 (2020): S145–S160 (doi). In 14,844 words from texts for grades 1 to 8, a vowel before two consonants was short in 78.8% of two-syllable words and 62.5% of longer ones (about 94% once weak vowels are set aside), while a vowel before one consonant was long in only 47.3% and 18.8%. Weak, unstressed vowels explain much of the gap. He concludes that dividing words by these rules costs more attention than it repays, and points to flexible trying of a vowel’s sounds, morphology and practice instead. The data are about the words, not about learners.
  • Shayne B. Piasta, Carol McDonald Connor, Barry J. Fishman and Frederick J. Morrison, “Teachers’ Knowledge of Literacy Concepts, Classroom Practices, and Student Reading Growth,” Scientific Studies of Reading 13, no. 3 (2009): 224–248. More time on explicit decoding went with bigger word-reading gains only when the teacher knew the content well.
  • Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil, “The Misunderstood Limits of Folk Science: An Illusion of Explanatory Depth,” Cognitive Science 26, no. 5 (2002): 521–562. People believe they understand how things work in more detail than they do, until they try to explain them: a reason to test understanding on new words rather than trust the feeling of it.
  • Thomas R. Kratochwill and colleagues, Single-Case Designs Technical Documentation (What Works Clearinghouse, 2010) (ERIC). Standards for evidence gathered with care from a few learners at a time.

Sentences

  • Randolph Quirk, Sidney Greenbaum, Geoffrey Leech and Jan Svartvik, A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language (Longman, 1985), for operator: their name for the first auxiliary, the word that carries the negative and moves to the front to make a question.
  • Zellig Harris, “Co-Occurrence and Transformation in Linguistic Structure,” Language 33, no. 3 (1957): 283–340, and Noam Chomsky, Syntactic Structures (Mouton, 1957), for kernel: their name for a language’s simplest sentences. The notation keeps the word for what’s built first, and grows the rest from it.

Copywrite

Learners come to language by copying it: the voices around them first, then letters and words, until the copies become something they can write for themselves. Every language user does the same, and so does anyone who builds on a language, whether in a lesson, a book or a notation like this.

Copyright guards a particular way of saying something. Copywrite is the older practice underneath it: taking what others have said and written, and writing it again in your own hand. This site is copywrite too, written in its own marks from what the people above worked out. The licence is meant to keep that open. Copy these cards, mark them, change them, and make them fit your learners.
