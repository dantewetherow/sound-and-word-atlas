Letters, names and sounds

The alphabet as the song sings it, one unit to a line. Tap a tile to show its letter, or the dot at the start of a line to show the whole unit. Tap the circle under a letter for its sound.

The alphabet

and

The circle under a consonant marks its sound. Under a vowel, the first tap gives the short sound and the cup; the second, the long sound, which is the letter’s name, and the long mark. A third tap clears it. Under c and g, the second tap makes them soft, as an e, i or y after them does (step 10). Under y, the first tap is the consonant of yes, the second the vowel of my (step 3). Under x, two sounds: /k/ and /s/.

Elemeno: L, M, N, O, P

The song gives H, I, J and K a beat each, then crowds L, M, N and O into two beats and holds P. Many learners hear one long word, elemeno. Give each letter its own beat while they learn it, and the letters come apart.

For teachers: why names, sounds and both cases

Names and sounds together. In a study of preschoolers taught letters for several weeks, those taught the names and the sounds together learned the letters’ names better than those taught only sounds, and learned at least as many sounds. Letters whose names hold their sound (b, d, f, m) were the easiest. The study didn’t follow the learners into reading (Piasta and Wagner 2010). The vowel names are their long sounds, so the alphabet song already gives the long vowels, and the sounds give the short ones: the first contrast on the route.

Uppercase, then lowercase. Among 461 preschoolers, a child who knew a letter’s uppercase form was more than 16 times as likely to know its lowercase form, and lowercase letters that look like their capitals (c, o, s) were known best (Turnbull et al. 2010). Learners seem to reach lowercase through uppercase. We know of no study showing that teaching lowercase first gives better or longer-lasting results. The time to spend is on the letters whose two forms look least alike: a, b, d, e, g, h, q and r.

Elemeno. The crowding of L, M, N, O and P is a teacher’s observation, not a research finding. Slowing the song costs nothing.
