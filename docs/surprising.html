Surprising connections

Each group of words here comes from one old root, but the words no longer look related. Sounds changed, and words came into English by different roads: from Latin, from Greek, or from English’s own past.

The word studies keep to words whose parts learners can see and build: experiment, experimental, experimentation. These groups are history: no word sum joins experiment to fear. They make good questions after a family is built: how could these be related?

*per-*ghel-*dwo-*deuk-sign and sealp and f, d and t

*per-: to try, to risk

Three roads into English. Experiment and peril came from Latin, through French. Pirate came from Greek through Latin, which spelled Greek ei with an i. Fear never left: English has had it since Old English. Its f is the p of the root, changed in the early Germanic languages (see the end of this page).

Sources: Indo-European Lexicon, per- 2e: experiment, peril, pirate, fear; Etymonline, fear: Old English fær, calamity, sudden danger, from the root *per-, to try, to risk. More on the *per- page and in the word study of experiment and experience.

*ghel-: to shine

All four are English from the start. In geolu, the g before e spelled the sound of y, and later spelling wrote it with a y. Many gl- words for light come from this root, which is part of why gl- and light go together.

Sources: Indo-European Lexicon, ghel- (to shine, glow; gold, yellow): gold, yellow, glow, gleam, glass. More on Sound and meaning: phonesthemes.

*dwo-: two

The w in two isn’t said, and the spelling keeps it: it is the w of twin, twice, twelve and between. Twig is from early Germanic *twigga, a fork.

Sources: Indo-European Lexicon, dwo-: two, twin, twelve, between, twig, twine; Etymonline, twig: Old English twig, from Proto-Germanic *twigga, a fork. More on the tw- words on Sound and meaning.

*deuk-: to lead, to pull

Latin kept the d of the root; early Germanic, and so English, changed it to t. So educate and duke lead, and tug, tow and team pull.

Sources: Indo-European Lexicon, deuk- (to pull): educate, duke, tug, tow, team; Etymonline, tug: Old English teohan, to pull, from the root *deuk-, to lead. More on the *deuk- page and in the word study From duct to education.

sign and seal

A seal is a little sign pressed into wax. French wore sigillum down to seel, and English took it without the g. Sign and signal kept theirs.

Sources: Indo-European Lexicon, seku̯-: Latin signum; Etymonline: sign, signal and seal. More on the sign page and in the word study The g in sign.

p and f, d and t

Two changes in the early Germanic languages were regular: p became f, and d became t. Latin kept the old sounds, and English borrowed Latin words later. So English has father beside paternal and tooth beside dentist, and the same change gave fear its f and tug its t.

Source: Grimm’s law, set out by Jacob Grimm in the second edition of his Deutsche Grammatik (1822). Each pair is in the Indo-European Lexicon under its root.
