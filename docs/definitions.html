The word

leg·o·mor·phic

/ˌlɛɡoʊˈmɔrfɪk/ leg-oh-MOR-fik adjective

  1. Having to do with legomorphs: the sounds, meaningful parts, and changes in spelling or sound that words are gathered from. A legomorphic reading of notation finds the e of note dropped at the seam and its job passed to the a of -ate.
  2. Having to do with legomorphics, the joining of a language's systems through speaking, reading and writing. Legomorphic Notation; legomorphic teaching.

Origin. From Greek λέγω (légō), “I gather, I pick out,” and so “I count, I tell, I say,” and Greek μορφή (morphḗ), “form, shape,” with -ic, “having to do with,” from Greek -ikós. Latin legō, “I gather, I choose, I read,” is the same word. Lego keeps the Greek verb whole, ending and all: λέγ-ω, “gather-I.” Coined by Dante Wetherow for the notation and the study it names.

Not from the toy. The word isn’t named for the LEGO brick, though the building fits. The LEGO Group’s name is short for the Danish leg godt, “play well” (the LEGO Group’s history).

Related. legomorph, legomorphics, Legomorphic Notation. From the same roots: dialogue, lexicon, legible, morpheme. See The word on the About page.

legomorph noun /ˈlɛɡoʊˌmɔrf/
  1. A phoneme, a morpheme, or a transformation in spelling or sound: the pieces and changes a word is gathered from, and so the things the notation marks. In note the legomorphs are its three phonemes, its one morpheme, and a transformation: the silent e reaching back to make the o long.
  2. In the grammar of legomorphics, a prime: one of the first categories a sentence is assembled from, such as the actor, the action and the acted-on (Latin primus, first).
Coined for the notation.
legomorphics noun, singular /ˌlɛɡoʊˈmɔrfɪks/
The integration of orthography, morphology, phonology, articulation, semantics and syntax through the legomorphic means: speaking, reading and writing, and the syntax of claims and questions. In plainer words, spelling, word parts, sounds, how the mouth makes them, meaning and word order, learned together through talk, reading and writing. Like phonics and linguistics, it takes a singular verb.Coined for the notation.
Legomorphic Notation noun
The marks and colours this site draws on ordinary spelling, so that a word’s legomorphs can be seen: its sounds, the letters that spell them, its meaningful parts, and the changes letters make to one another. In a sentence, its colours show actors, actions and operators. The marks are listed below and drawn in Reading the marks.Coined for the notation.

Sentences

Every sentence has a head, a body and legs: who or what it’s about, what they do, and what they do it to. These are the words for its parts, and for the two things a sentence can do, claim and ask.

kernel noun
The small core a sentence is built from: an actor, an operator and an action, with an acted-on when there is one. On the route, a step's kernel words are the short, frequent words that fill those places first: the forms of be, have and do, the modals, and verbs like go. Learners over-practise the kernel in claims and questions, then grow it: She could go. She could go up the hill.Zellig Harris and Noam Chomsky called a language's simplest sentences its kernel in 1957, the sentences the others were transformed from. Here the kernel is what's built first, and the rest grows onto it in time.
actor noun
Who or what the sentence is about, usually the one doing the action. In the picture of a sentence as a head, a body and legs, the actor is the head. Green in the sentence colours: she in She could go.
action noun
What the actor does: the body. Red in the sentence colours: go in She could go. A sentence with a form of be and no other verb, like She is here, has no action.
acted-on noun
Who or what the action is done to: the legs. Green, like the actor: the note in She could read the note. Not every sentence has one. She could go doesn't.
operator noun
The first helping verb in a sentence: the word that carries not and moves to the front to make a question, as could does in She could go and Could she go? Operators are forms of be, have and do, and the modals. Blue, but only in the forms that can move to the front, like is, has and did. Been, being and done never move, so they take the verb's red.Adopted from Randolph Quirk, Sidney Greenbaum, Geoffrey Leech and Jan Svartvik, A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language (Longman, 1985).
modal noun
An operator that says how sure, able, allowed or bound the claim is: can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might and must. The nine modals are always operators, so they're always blue. The semi-modals (have to, ought to, going to, used to) don't move to the front, so they stay red.
query noun
A question word: who, what, where, when, why or how. A query asks for more than yes or no, and it comes before the operator: Where could she go? The name keeps it apart from the operator.
claim noun
A sentence that says something is so: She could go. I am.
question noun
A sentence that asks. A yes-or-no question puts the operator in front of the actor: Could she go? Am I? A query question starts with a query: Where could she go?
flip noun and verb
Moving the operator across the actor, to turn a claim into a question and back: She could go. Could she go? On the word cards, the card that moves is outlined in blue.

Marks

The marks Legomorphic Notation draws, with a word that shows each one. Their names are the notation’s own. Reading the marks has the same marks with the legend’s drawings.

sound circle
One circle under each sound: red for a vowel sound, black for a consonant, green for sh, th, ch.
syllable swoop
One swoop under each syllable.
short mark
A cup over a vowel that keeps its short sound.
long mark
A bar over a vowel that says its name.
bond bar
A bar under letters that spell one sound: black for consonant units and for doubled letters, boxed or not, green for sh, th, ch, red for vowel units.
r-controlled unit
A red bar under a vowel and its r, with the vowel red, the r black and one circle.
block box
A box around the vowel protector at work: a pair or cluster with a vowel after it, which it stops from reaching back. A pair keeps its bond bar when the box comes; a cluster has none.
cone of silence
A dotted ring around a silent e.
vowel vault
An arc from an e, or a vowel after one consonant, back to the vowel it makes long.
nice-nudge
A small arc under the letters from e, i or y to the c or g it softens.
blocked vault
A half-arc that ends in a red X where a box stops the vault.
morpheme seam
A dotted line between morphemes.
sender
The letter a vowel vault comes from: a silent e, or a vowel after one consonant, like the a of -ate, which sends the o of notate its vault.
red apostrophe
A red apostrophe in a contraction, in place of the vowel it drops.

Teaching

learning loop noun
The teaching cycle. Learners move among seeing, listening, speaking, reading and writing: a clean pass, then back to the start, back to any earlier mode, or between any two. A pass can swap a part (a sound, its articulation, an actor, an action, an operator, a preposition) to fit the learner's need, interest and skill in the moment.Coined for the notation.
analog noun
A known word used as the model for words that share its spelling: would for could and should. An analog shares its letters with the words it leads to, which a rhyme doesn't have to do.
route noun
This site's scope and sequence: 32 steps through English spelling, with Edward Carney's A Survey of English Spelling as its backbone. See the route.
word sum noun
A word written as its morphemes joined by plus signs, with an arrow to the word they make: not(e) + ate → notate. A letter in brackets drops at the seam.Adopted from Melvyn Ramsden's Real Spelling. Pete Bowers brought word sums into classrooms through structured word inquiry. See Credits.
copywrite noun
The older practice underneath copyright: taking what others have said and written, and writing it again in your own hand. It's what children do as they learn to talk, read and write, and what this site does with the writers it builds on.Coined for the notation. See Credits.
