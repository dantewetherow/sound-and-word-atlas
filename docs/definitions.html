Definitions

The words of the notation

The word

leg·o·mor·phic

/ˌlɛɡoʊˈmɔrfɪk/ leg-oh-MOR-fik adjective

  1. Having to do with legomorphs: the sounds, meaningful parts, and changes in spelling or sound that words are gathered from. A legomorphic reading of notation finds the e of note dropped at the seam and its job passed to the a of -ate.
  2. Having to do with legomorphics, the joining of a language's systems through speaking, reading and writing. Legomorphic Notation; legomorphic teaching.

Origin. From legō, a verb Greek and Latin both inherited from the root *leg-, to gather. Greek λέγω (légō) is “I gather, I pick out,” and so “I count, I tell, I say”: the speaker’s sense. Latin legō is “I gather, I choose, I read”: the reader’s sense. In both, the -ō is the ending for “I,” so lego is leg-ō, “gather-I,” as Latin veto is “I forbid.” Then Greek μορφή (morphḗ), “form, shape,” and -ic, “having to do with,” from Greek -ikós through Latin -icus. Coined by Dante Wetherow for the notation and the study it names.

Not from the toy. The word isn’t named for the LEGO brick, though the building fits. The LEGO Group’s name is short for the Danish leg godt, “play well” (the LEGO Group’s history).

Related. legomorph, legomorphics, Legomorphic Notation. From the same roots: dialogue, lexicon, legible, morpheme. See The word on the About page, and the poets’ uses of both verbs in Legō in Latin and in Greek.

legomorph noun /ˈlɛɡoʊˌmɔrf/
  1. A phoneme, a morpheme, or a transformation in spelling or sound: the pieces and changes a word is gathered from, and so the things the notation marks. In note the legomorphs are its three phonemes, its one morpheme, and a transformation: the silent e reaching back to make the o long.
  2. In the grammar of legomorphics, a prime: one of the first categories a sentence is assembled from, such as the actor, the action and the acted-on (Latin primus, first).
Coined for the notation.
legomorphics noun, singular /ˌlɛɡoʊˈmɔrfɪks/
The integration of orthography, morphology, phonology, articulation, semantics and syntax through the legomorphic means: speaking, reading and writing, and the syntax of claims and questions. In plainer words, spelling, word parts, sounds, how the mouth makes them, meaning and word order, learned together through talk, reading and writing. Like phonics and linguistics, it takes a singular verb.Coined for the notation.
Legomorphic Notation noun
The marks and colours this site draws on ordinary spelling, so that a word’s legomorphs can be seen: its sounds, the letters that spell them, its meaningful parts, and the changes letters make to one another. The marks are listed below and drawn in Notations.Coined for the notation.

The system

The units of speech, spelling and meaning, from one speech sound to what a sentence means. Each comes with the field that studies it and who named it. How it fits together draws them as rings, each defined by the rings around it.

phone noun
A single speech sound, as it is actually said. Phones are written in square brackets: [p], [t]. On the rings.From Greek phōnē, voice or sound, the root of phonics, phoneme and telephone. No one person named it. Benjamin Lee Whorf coined allophone around 1929 for the phones that count as one phoneme.
phonetics noun
The study of phones: how the mouth makes them, how they travel through the air, and how the ear takes them in. On the rings.From Greek phōnētikos, of speaking. The International Phonetic Alphabet, first published in 1888, gives each phone one symbol.
phoneme noun
A speech sound that tells words apart in one language. Change it and the word changes: kit and kid. Phonemes are written between slashes: /t/, /d/. On the rings.Antoni Dufriche-Desgenettes was writing French phonème for a speech sound by 1865, though it is usually dated 1873. Jan Baudouin de Courtenay and Mikołaj Kruszewski, at Kazan in the 1870s and 1880s, gave it its modern sense. Nikolai Trubetzkoy defined it as the smallest unit that tells words apart (Grundzüge der Phonologie, 1939).
phonology noun
The study of a language’s phonemes: which sounds it tells apart, and the orders they may come in. On the rings.Trubetzkoy and the Prague linguists made phonology a field of its own in the 1920s and 1930s, building on the Kazan school.
grapheme noun
A letter or team of letters that spells one phoneme: the sh, the i and the p of ship. A grapheme can be one letter, two (sh), three (igh) or four (the ough of thorough). On the rings.Baudouin de Courtenay is usually credited with the word, early in the twentieth century, for the mind’s picture of a letter. Aarni Penttilä used it in 1932, and Benjamin Lee Whorf and W. Freeman Twaddell in the 1930s. Two senses are in use. Phonics, and this site, use the first: letters that spell a phoneme. Some writing-system research uses the smallest unit that tells written words apart.
orthography noun
A language’s spelling system: its graphemes, and the patterns for which one to use where. English orthography spells sounds, and it also keeps the parts of words looking the same. On the rings.From Greek orthos, straight or correct, and graphia, writing: correct writing.
morpheme noun
The smallest part of a word that carries meaning: a base like sign, a prefix like re-, a suffix like -al. On the rings.Baudouin de Courtenay and Kruszewski worked out the idea at Kazan in the early 1880s, alongside the phoneme. Antoine Meillet’s French morphème spread it. Leonard Bloomfield made it the smallest unit of meaning in Language (1933).
morphology noun
The study of morphemes: how words are built from parts, and how a family of words shares a base. On the rings.Johann Wolfgang von Goethe coined Morphologie for the study of living forms, in notes from 1796. August Schleicher applied it to language in 1859.
word noun
A unit that can stand alone in speech and in writing. In print it sits between spaces. On the rings.Leonard Bloomfield called the word a minimum free form: the smallest form that can be said on its own (Language, 1933).
sentence noun
Words put in order to make a claim or ask a question. On the rings.
syntax noun
The study of how words are put in order to make sentences. On the rings.From Greek syntaxis, an arranging. Apollonius Dyscolus wrote the first Greek book on it, On Syntax, in the second century.
meaning noun
What a part, a word or a sentence says, and what it does when someone uses it. On the rings.
semantics noun
The study of meaning. The study of meaning in use, in context, is pragmatics. On the rings.Michel Bréal named it, French sémantique, in 1883. Charles Morris (1938) set semantics beside syntax and pragmatics.
morphophonemics noun
How a morpheme’s sounds change when it joins other morphemes. Also called morphophonology, or morphonology. On the rings.Baudouin de Courtenay studied these alternations in 1894. Nikolai Trubetzkoy proposed morphonology as its own chapter of linguistics in 1929. Leonard Bloomfield’s Menomini Morphophonemics (1939) gave the English name. Noam Chomsky and Morris Halle (1968) argued that English spelling is close to optimal because it keeps morphemes whole. Later studies find that claim goes too far.
orthographic mapping noun
How a reader stores a word: by connecting its graphemes to its phonemes, so its spelling, pronunciation and meaning are kept together. On the rings.The term and the account are Linnea Ehri’s, from her research on how readers learn words by sight. Her 2014 paper sets them out.

Sentences

Every sentence has a head, a body and legs: who or what it’s about, what they do, and what they do it to. These are the words for its parts, and for the two things a sentence can do, claim and ask.

kernel noun
The small core a sentence is built from: an actor, an operator and an action, with an acted-on when there is one. On the path, a step's kernel words are the short, frequent words that fill those places first: the forms of be, have and do, the modals, and verbs like go. Learners over-practise the kernel in claims and questions, then grow it: She could go. She could go up the hill.Zellig Harris and Noam Chomsky called a language's simplest sentences its kernel in 1957, the sentences the others were transformed from. Here the kernel is what's built first, and the rest grows onto it in time.
actor noun
Who or what the sentence is about, usually the one doing the action. In the picture of a sentence as a head, a body and legs, the actor is the head: she in She could go.
action noun
What the actor does: the body, as go is in She could go. A sentence with a form of be and no other verb, like She is here, has no action.
acted-on noun
Who or what the action is done to: the legs, as the note is in She could read the note. Not every sentence has one. She could go doesn't.
operator noun
The first helping verb in a sentence: the word that carries not and moves to the front to make a question, as could does in She could go and Could she go? Operators are the modals and the forms of be, have and do that can move to the front, like is, has and did. Been, being and done never move, so they are never operators.Adopted from Randolph Quirk, Sidney Greenbaum, Geoffrey Leech and Jan Svartvik, A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language (Longman, 1985).
modal noun
An operator that says how sure, able, allowed or bound the claim is: can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might and must. The nine modals are always operators. The semi-modals (have to, ought to, going to, used to) mean what modals mean but don't move to the front themselves: Do we have to go? Are we going to go?
query noun
A question word: who, what, where, when, why or how. A query asks for more than yes or no, and it comes before the operator: Where could she go? The name keeps it apart from the operator.
claim noun
A sentence that says something is so: She could go. I am.
question noun
A sentence that asks. A yes-or-no question puts the operator in front of the actor: Could she go? Am I? A query question starts with a query: Where could she go?
flip noun and verb
Moving the operator across the actor, to turn a claim into a question and back: She could go. Could she go? On the word cards, the card that moves is outlined.

Marks

The marks Legomorphic Notation draws, each on one word with that mark alone. Their names are the notation’s own. The marks page has them as a legend.

sound circle
One circle under each sound: red for a vowel sound, black for a consonant, green for sh, th, ch and the sh of -tion.
syllable swoop
One swoop under each syllable.
short mark
A cup over a vowel that keeps its short sound.
long mark
A bar over a vowel that spells its name.
bond bar
A bar under letters that work as one unit, most of them spelling one sound: black for consonant units and for doubled letters, boxed or not, green for sh, th, ch and the ti of -tion, red for vowel units. The letters keep their own colours. Qu is the consonant unit with two sounds: the bar ties the q to the u it always comes with, and a circle under each letter counts the k and the w.
r-controlled unit
A red bar under a vowel and its r, with the vowel red, the r black and one circle.
block box
A box around the vowel protector at work: a pair or cluster with a vowel after it, which it stops from reaching back. A pair keeps its bond bar when the box comes; a cluster has none.
cone of silence
A dotted ring around a silent letter: the e of have, and a consonant that another form of its word says aloud, the g of sign (signal). Other silent consonants join their unit: the kn of knife. “All letters are ‘silent’, but some are more silent than others” (Carney 1994: 40): the g of sign speaks up in signal.
schwa
A small ə over a vowel said weakly, one for the whole unit: the a of about, the ou of famous. The symbol is Johann Andreas Schmeller’s, from his work on Bavarian speech in the 1820s.
borrowed sound
A vowel spelling another vowel’s sound, with that sound written small over it: oo over the o of do, u over the o of come, ah over the a of want, ee over the y of happy. The oo of book and put is written ʊ, the International Phonetic Alphabet’s letter for it, so it isn’t read as the oo of moon. After Romalda Spalding’s marking (1957), which numbers a letter’s sounds over it.
vowel vault
An arc from the letter that makes a vowel long back to it: a silent e, a y, a vowel in a dropped e’s place, or an i before a vowel.
nice-nudge
A small arc under the letters from e, i or y to the c or g it softens.
blocked vault
A half-arc that ends in a red X where a box stops the vault.
morpheme seam
A dotted line between morphemes.
weak and strong forms
One spelling, two ways of saying it: the is weak in the cat and strong in the apple or THE place to be; a, to and of work the same way. The weak form takes the ə; the strong form, used for emphasis, at the end of a phrase, or before a vowel sound, takes its full vowel. The word walk shows both side by side.
sender
The letter a vowel vault comes from: a silent e, including the e of -le (table, blocked by the double in bubble); a y; a vowel that takes a dropped e’s place, like the a of -ate, which sends the o of notate its vault; or an i before another vowel, as in experience. A long vowel no letter causes (over, tiger) has no sender, and no vault.
red apostrophe
A red apostrophe in a contraction, in place of the vowel it drops.

Teaching

learning loop noun
The teaching cycle. Learners move among seeing, listening, speaking, reading and writing: a clean pass, then back to the start, back to any earlier mode, or between any two. A pass can swap a part (a sound, its articulation, an actor, an action, an operator, a preposition) to fit the learner's need, interest and skill in the moment.Coined for the notation.
analog noun
A known word used as the model for words that share its spelling: would for could and should. An analog shares its letters with the words it leads to, which a rhyme doesn't have to do.
path noun
This site's scope and sequence: 32 steps through English spelling, with Edward Carney's A Survey of English Spelling as its backbone. Called the route until 14 November 2026. See the path.
word sum noun
A word written as its morphemes joined by plus signs, with an arrow to the word they make: not(e) + ate → notate. A letter in brackets drops at the seam.Adopted from Melvyn Ramsden's Real Spelling. Pete Bowers brought word sums into classrooms through structured word inquiry. See Credits.
copywrite noun
The older practice underneath copyright: taking what others have said and written, and writing it again in your own hand. It's what every learner does on the way to talking, reading and writing, and what this site does with the writers it builds on.Coined for the notation. See Credits.

Studying language

Two ways to look at any word: as it is now, or as it has changed. Each comes with an everyday word that can stand in for it.

synchronic adjective
Looking at a language as it is at one time, the way it's used now, without its history. A synchronic reading of -ion asks what the ending does in the words in use today: action, notation, explanation. In everyday words, a snapshot.Adopted from Ferdinand de Saussure, whose Course in General Linguistics (1916) set it against diachronic. From Greek syn, “together,” and khronos, “time”: belonging to the same time.
diachronic adjective
Looking at a language through time, at how its words, sounds and spellings have changed. A diachronic reading of before finds Old English beforan, where be, “by,” was joined to foran, “from the front,” and finds for and fore growing from one root. In everyday words, a time-lapse.Adopted from Ferdinand de Saussure, as synchronic is. From Greek dia, “through,” and khronos, “time.”
free adjective
Said of a morpheme that can be a word on its own: fun in funny, sun in sunny. A seam after a free base is easy to see. In everyday words, it stands alone.Adopted from Leonard Bloomfield, whose Language (1933) set free forms, which can be said alone, against bound forms, which can’t.
bound adjective
Said of a morpheme that is never a word on its own: endings like -y and -ness, and bases that have stopped being said alone, like hap in happy and mishap. A family of words is what shows a bound base. See hap. In everyday words, it needs a partner.Adopted from Leonard Bloomfield, as free is.
spells its name phrase
Said of a vowel letter spelling the long sound that is also its letter name: the a of cake, the i of like. Many phonics programs say the vowel “says its name”. On this site letters spell sounds and people say them, so a vowel spells its name, a team spells its second sound, and a c before e spells /s/.Adapted from the classroom phrase “says its name”. Changed across the site 21 November 2026.
phonestheme noun
A sound many words with a shared meaning have in common: gl- in glow, gleam and glitter (light), sn- in sniff, snout and sneeze (the nose). It isn’t a morpheme, since the rest of the word means nothing on its own, and it isn’t a rule: glove and snow have the letters and not the meaning. See Sound and meaning and the word study.Coined by J. R. Firth (Speech, 1930), from Greek phōnē, sound, and aisthēma, what is felt.
