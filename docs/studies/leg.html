leg: the oldest meaning is gather

coltogether+lectgathered

To collect is to gather things together.

The root *leg- meant to gather, to collect. Latin made it legere, and after a prefix its past form is -lectus, which gives English lect: col + lect, where col is con, together, made like the l after it.

Gather the ones you want, and you choose

pick out
choose by voting
gather together
something chosen to learn

Gathering turns into choosing.

se, apart; e, out (ex before l): select is to gather apart, elect to gather out. Latin used legere for choosing as well as gathering. Lesson comes up again on the reading road.

One word, two languages

legōgather + I: I gather

Latinlegō

I pick outfootprints, going back the way you came

I readletters, gathered with your eyes

Greekλέγω

I countseals on a beach, one by one

I saywords, laid out in order

The same word: read in Latin, say in Greek.

Greek and Latin both have legō; the ō is the ending for I, as in Latin veto, I forbid. Virgil uses it for a man picking up his own footprints (Aeneid 9.392–393); Homer, for the sea god Proteus counting his seals (Odyssey 4.451). The next two slides follow each road into English. More lines are in Legō in Latin and in Greek, with the poets’ uses for the dying, for teachers.

In Latin, legere came to mean read.

Latin legere: gather, choose, read. To read is to gather the letters with your eyes. Lecture is lect + ure; legend is leg + end, Latin legenda, things to be read; legible is leg + ible. The g says j before e and i.

In Greek, legein came to mean say.

Greek legō: I gather, I count, I say: to lay words in order. Greek gave lexis, a word, and logos, a word, an account, reason: lex, log, logue. Dialogue and prologue are set in type: the site has no checked marks for -logue yet.

Gather, choose, read, say: all leg.

Speakers of Greek and Latin carried the root into words about words: gathering words is choosing them, reading them and saying them. English borrowed the family again and again, by many roads.

A legend is something to read

legread+endto be

From the end: to be read. On a map, the legend is the key you read to understand the marks.

Medieval Latin legenda, things to be read, first the lives of saints read aloud. Later a legend was also the words written on a coin or a map, and so the key to a map’s marks. This site was called A Legend for English Words for a week in November 2026.

Words wear down as they travel

lectioLatin: a reading

leçonFrench: the c and t worn away

A lesson was first a reading.

Latin lectio, a reading (lect + ion), became Old French leçon, and English lesson. The spelling no longer shows lect, but the family still holds it: a lesson, a lecture, a lectern to read from.

Build the notation’s name

leggather+oI+morphform+icto do with

From the end: to do with the forms I gather. Legomorphic: how sounds and letters gather into words.

Word sum: leg + o + morph + ic → legomorphic. The o is the ending for I in Greek and Latin alike, so legō is I gather, as Latin veto is I forbid; morph is Greek morphē, form. The verb in both languages, with the poets’ lines, is in Legō in Latin and in Greek.

Sounds and words, assembled

With -s, the name of the study, as in phonics and mathematics.

Gathering sounds into words, and words into sentences: assembling them.

-ics names a field of study (phonics, mathematics, linguistics). The site’s name, Sounds and Words, Assembled, says the same thing in other words: assemble is another root, *sem-, together.

A word that only looks like it

LEGO

The name of the bricks is Danish: leg godt, play well. A different word, from a different place.

But a good picture: pieces that click together to make something new.

The toy company took its name from Danish leg godt, play well, in the 1930s. Danish lege, to play, is not from *leg-. Look-alikes are worth naming, so learners test a family by meaning as well as by letters.

For teachers

Word sums: col + lect → collect; se + lect → select; lect + ure → lecture; leg + end → legend; leg + ible → legible; lex + ic + on → lexicon; log + ic → logic; leg + o + morph + ic → legomorphic.

The family follows Etymonline and the Indo-European Lexicon on *leg-, with Wiktionary on Greek légō and Latin legō.
