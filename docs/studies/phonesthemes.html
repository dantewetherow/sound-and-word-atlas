Growl like a dog: grr

What does it mean?

Most people say anger, or a warning. You can tell from the sound.

Let the class growl. Grr is an interjection, like shh and ouch: its sound suggests its meaning. This study is about a related pattern inside ordinary words: groups of words that share a sound and a meaning.

Words that end in -owl

All five are angry sounds or looks, or creeping up to hunt.

same letters, a different sound: not in the group

-owl: growl, howl, yowl, scowl, prowl, and the owl that hunts at night. Bowl spells the o of snow with its ow, so it doesn’t sound like the others and isn’t in the group. A phonestheme is a shared sound; the spelling usually shows it.

Words that start with gl-

What do they share? Light, and seeing.

Every one of these is about light: glow, gleam, glint, glitter, glimmer, glisten. Add glare, glance and glimpse for seeing. In one count of written English, about 4 in 10 different gl- words had to do with light or seeing, and 6 in 10 of their uses (Bergen 2004).

Take the gl away

gl + owow?

gl + eameam?

gl + itteritter?

What’s left means nothing, so gl- is not a morpheme. A shared sound like this is a phonestheme.

Here is the line between a phonestheme and a morpheme. A word sum needs every part to carry meaning: re + play, sign + al. With gl-, the rest of the word means nothing, so there is no sum. J. R. Firth named this in 1930: a phonestheme, from Greek phōnē, sound, and aisthēma, what is felt.

Not every gl word

Many gl- words are about light. Not all of them are.

A glove, glue and a globe don’t shine. Every cluster has words like these. Showing them keeps learners from treating the pattern as a rule.

Many gl- light words share a root

*ghel-a very old word: to shine

These words all come from one old root meaning to shine.

The Indo-European root *ghel-, to shine, is behind gleam, glow, glass, glare, glimmer, glitter and glisten. It is also behind gold and yellow (Indo-European Lexicon, University of Texas, after Pokorny). So the gl- light words are partly one word family and partly words that joined the group later.

Words that start with sn-

The nose.

fnese →

the Old English word changed its first sound

Sneeze was fnēsan in Old English, and should have come down as fnese. The fn- dropped out and sn- took its place, perhaps because sn- suited the nose (Anatoly Liberman, OUPblog, 2019). So a group can change the spelling of a word that joins it. Snail, snake and snow don’t fit.

Words that start with sl-

Sliding and slipping. But sleep and slim are not.

sl- was Firth’s own first example. A count of the whole dictionary didn’t back it up (Otis and Sagi 2008): there are plenty of sl- words that don’t slide. Many readers notice the pattern; the numbers do not support it.

Words that start with sw- and tw-

sw-: swinging and sweeping

tw-: twisting

sw- words name swinging, sweeping or circling movements: swing, sway, sweep, swirl, swoop, swish. tw- words name twisting: twist, twirl, twine, twitch, tweak. Sweet, swan and swamp don’t swing.

tw- and two

These come from the old word for two. So does twist: two strands.

Indo-European *dwo-, two, gives two, twin, twelve, twenty, twice, between, and twig (where a branch splits). Twist and twine are in the family too, as thread of two strands. Twirl, tweak and twitch share only the sound and a related meaning. Between is be + tween, “by two”, marked with its seam like before and behind.

Words that end in -ash and -ump

-ash: a hard, fast hit

-ump: a rounded lump

And wash? The w changes the a’s sound. It isn’t in the group.

-ash: bash, crash, smash, splash, clash, dash, flash. -ump: bump, lump, clump, stump, plump. Cash and ash don’t hit; jump and pump aren’t lumps. In wash the a spells a different sound after w, so it falls out of the cluster altogether.

What an experiment found

People saw pairs like glitter and glow, then read the second word.

This was faster than for pairs sharing only letters or meaning.

Readers connect words that share both a sound and a meaning.

Benjamin Bergen, “The psychological reality of phonaesthemes,” Language 80 (2004): a priming test with gl- and sn- pairs. Pairs that shared a phonestheme were read faster than pairs that shared only letters or only meaning. Otis and Sagi (2008) then counted the whole dictionary: gl-, sn-, sw-, tw-, str-, -ash and -ump held up; sl- didn’t.

Make one up

A glimp is probably… a tiny flash of light

A snoffle is probably… a small, quick sniff

Your turn: a gl- word, an sn- word, an -ash word. What do they mean?

Made-up words test the pattern: learners often agree on what a glimp might be without being told. People coining new words follow these clusters too. Keep the words silly, not awkward.

For teachers

A phonestheme is a sound many words with a shared meaning have in common. It isn’t a morpheme: the rest of the word means nothing alone, so there is no word sum. It isn’t a rule either: every cluster has words with the letters but not the meaning. Some clusters are partly old families (gl- from *ghel-, to shine; tw- from *dwo-, two).

Sources: J. R. Firth, Speech (1930); Bolinger (1949); Marchand (1959); Bergen, Language 80 (2004); Otis and Sagi (2008); Liberman, OUPblog (2019). The ten clusters, each word marked, are on Sound and meaning.
