Two long words. What’s the same?

experiment

experience

The same start. A different end.

Both are ex + peri, with a different ending: -ment and -ence. The next three slides give each part a felt meaning from words learners know, before the parts go back together.

ex- means out.

Latin ex, out of; in some words, all the way, thoroughly. Before some letters it shrinks to e-: educate, emerge. The examples are set in type: the site has no checked marks for them yet.

-ment: the doing of it, or what comes of it.

Latin -mentum: the act, the means or the result. It makes a noun from a verb.

-ent tells what something is like. -ence turns it into a thing you can have.

Show the job, not a definition: -ent describes, -ence names. Latin’s present form, -ens (patiens, enduring; absens, being away), and its noun, -entia. ‘Have’ is a handle, not a rule: we sat in silence, we noticed his absence. Check by ear: The room was ___ (silent). We sat in ___ (silence).

Is -ment just -ent with an m?

An idea to test: the m keeps two vowels apart.

a vowel before it

a consonant before it

agreement

government

fluent

different

-ment comes after both. So does -ent, with no m.

-ment and -ent are two suffixes with two jobs.

If the m kept two vowels apart, -ment would come only after vowels, and -ent never would. Both come after both. Experiment and experience settle it: both endings follow the same i. Latin -mentum made nouns for the means or the result of a verb: monēre, to warn, monumentum. Latin -ēns, -entis was the present form of a verb, and -entia its noun. The two look alike in Latin too; their histories are separate.

Build it

exout+peritry+mentthe doing

Read it from the end: the doing of trying it out. An experiment is a trying-out.

Word sum: ex + peri + ment → experiment. Read from the last part back, the parts give the meaning in order. The suffix tells you what kind of word it is (a thing, an act). The rest tells you what it is about.

And the other one

exout+peritry+encea thing you have

From the end: what you have from trying things out. Experience is what trying leaves you with.

Word sum: ex + peri + ence → experience. -ence names, as on the -ence slide: she has experience; the word that describes her is experienced. In Latin, experientia was the trying and the knowing that comes of it. English once used the two words almost the same way; French still uses one, expérience, for both.

The e is short in one word and long in the other

The e is short:
ex-PAIR-i-ment

The i makes the e long:
ex-PEER-i-ence

Some say ex-PEER-i-ment too: same parts, same spelling.

An i before a vowel makes the vowel before it long (period, serious); before a consonant it doesn’t (peril, merit). The vault in experience draws it. Merriam-Webster gives ik-SPER-i-ment, also ik-SPIR-i-ment: mark what the learner says. More on the teacher’s page.

And the i?

experiment

experience

Both words have an i after per. It’s left over from the old Latin word.

You can read both words without knowing why the i is there.

The i is Latin’s theme vowel: experīrī is a verb of the class whose stems end in ī (audīre: audience, auditor). It marks the verb’s class and carries no meaning. Expert, from expertus, has none: the root is per. In experience it also does a job in sound, making the e long. None of this is needed to teach the morphology, phonology, spelling or history here.

Keep building

+ al: to do with experiments
+ ation: the doing of experiments
+ er: one who does the experiment

Each ending makes a new kind of word.

Word sums: experiment + al → experimental; experiment + ation → experimentation; experiment + er → experimenter. English has no experimentate, so -ation is written whole. Read from the end: -al describes, -ion names a doing, -er names the one who does it.

Say them. The beat moves.

ex·PER·i·ment

ex·per·i·MEN·tal

ex·per·i·men·TA·tion

In experiment, the e of ment is hard to hear.

In experimental, it’s a clear short e.

Not sure of a vowel? Say a longer word in the family.

-al and -ion pull the stress to the syllable just before them: exPERiment, experiMENtal, experimenTAtion. The e of ment is weak (a schwa) in experiment. In experimental it is stressed, a short e. In experimentation people say it both ways (Merriam-Webster). The spelling is ment in all three, so the word with the stress on that syllable tells you which vowel to write. The same test works for -ence: experience, experiential.

Build on experience

experience, experiential: where does the t come from?

Word sums: experienc(e) + ed → experienced; in + experienc(e) + ed → inexperienced; ex + peri + ent + ial → experiential. The e at the end of experience drops before -ed. In experiential the stress lands on en, and the e is a clear short e.

In -ence, the c spells /s/. In -ential, ti spells /sh/.

-ence and -ential both grow from -ent.

Latin’s present form ended in -ent- (confidens, confidentis). Its noun added -ia (confidentia), and the describing word -ialis. English spells the noun’s end -ence, with c for /s/, and keeps the t before -ial. English has no experient, but Latin had experiens, so the t is in experiential all the same. Word sums: confid + ent + ial, resid + ent + ial, ex + peri + ent + ial.

For teachers

Word sums: ex + peri + ment → experiment; ex + peri + ence → experience; experiment + al → experimental; experiment + ation → experimentation; experiment + er → experimenter; in + experienc(e) + ed → inexperienced; ex + peri + ent + ial → experiential.

-ment and -ent are two suffixes: Latin -mentum (Wiktionary), and the present form -ēns, -entis, with its noun -entia (Peter Smith, Greek and Latin Roots, §82).
