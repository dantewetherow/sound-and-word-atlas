How it fits together

Speech, spelling and meaning are layers of one system. Each ring here is one layer, from a single speech sound at the centre to meaning at the edge.

Choose a ring to read what it is, how it fits the rings beside it, and who named it. The dotted lines are bridges that cross the rings.

The rings are an order, not boxes inside boxes. A grapheme doesn’t hold phonemes; it spells them. A morpheme is said in phonemes and spelled in graphemes.

The centre

phone noun

What it isA single speech sound, as it is actually said. Phones are written in square brackets: [p], [t].

How it fitsPhones are the centre: what the mouth makes and the ear takes in. Every ring outside is built on them. Many phones can count as one phoneme.

Who named itFrom Greek phōnē, voice or sound, the root of phonics, phoneme and telephone. No one person named it. Benjamin Lee Whorf coined allophone around 1929 for the phones that count as one phoneme.

Sources: Phone (phonetics); Allophone.

phonetics noun

What it isThe study of phones: how the mouth makes them, how they travel through the air, and how the ear takes them in.

Who named itFrom Greek phōnētikos, of speaking. The International Phonetic Alphabet, first published in 1888, gives each phone one symbol.

Ring 2 from the centre

phoneme noun

What it isA speech sound that tells words apart in one language. Change it and the word changes: kit and kid. Phonemes are written between slashes: /t/, /d/.

How it fitsA phoneme gathers the phones a language treats as one sound. It sits between those phones and the graphemes that spell it.

Who named itAntoni Dufriche-Desgenettes was writing French phonème for a speech sound by 1865, though it is usually dated 1873. Jan Baudouin de Courtenay and Mikołaj Kruszewski, at Kazan in the 1870s and 1880s, gave it its modern sense. Nikolai Trubetzkoy defined it as the smallest unit that tells words apart (Grundzüge der Phonologie, 1939).

Sources: Joachim Mugdan, “On the origins of the term phoneme,” Historiographia Linguistica 38 (2011); Jan Baudouin de Courtenay.

phonology noun

What it isThe study of a language’s phonemes: which sounds it tells apart, and the orders they may come in.

Who named itTrubetzkoy and the Prague linguists made phonology a field of its own in the 1920s and 1930s, building on the Kazan school.

Sources: Nikolai Trubetzkoy.

Ring 3 from the centre

grapheme noun

What it isA letter or team of letters that spells one phoneme: the sh, the i and the p of ship. A grapheme can be one letter, two (sh), three (igh) or four (the ough of thorough).

How it fitsGraphemes are where speech meets the page. A grapheme doesn’t hold phonemes; it spells them. Strung together, graphemes spell morphemes.

Who named itBaudouin de Courtenay is usually credited with the word, early in the twentieth century, for the mind’s picture of a letter. Aarni Penttilä used it in 1932, and Benjamin Lee Whorf and W. Freeman Twaddell in the 1930s. Two senses are in use. Phonics, and this site, use the first: letters that spell a phoneme. Some writing-system research uses the smallest unit that tells written words apart.

Sources: Dimitrios Meletis, “The grapheme as a universal basic unit of writing,” Writing Systems Research (2019).

orthography noun

What it isA language’s spelling system: its graphemes, and the patterns for which one to use where. English orthography spells sounds, and it also keeps the parts of words looking the same.

Who named itFrom Greek orthos, straight or correct, and graphia, writing: correct writing.

Sources: Etymonline, orthography.

Ring 4 from the centre

morpheme noun

What it isThe smallest part of a word that carries meaning: a base like sign, a prefix like re-, a suffix like -al.

How it fitsA morpheme is said in phonemes and spelled in graphemes. Morphemes join at seams to make words, and a word’s meaning is built from theirs.

Who named itBaudouin de Courtenay and Kruszewski worked out the idea at Kazan in the early 1880s, alongside the phoneme. Antoine Meillet’s French morphème spread it. Leonard Bloomfield made it the smallest unit of meaning in Language (1933).

Sources: Historiographia Linguistica 28, on the morpheme at Kazan; LINGUIST List, the early sense of morpheme (2011).

morphology noun

What it isThe study of morphemes: how words are built from parts, and how a family of words shares a base.

Who named itJohann Wolfgang von Goethe coined Morphologie for the study of living forms, in notes from 1796. August Schleicher applied it to language in 1859.

Sources: wordorigins.org, morphology.

Ring 5 from the centre

word noun

What it isA unit that can stand alone in speech and in writing. In print it sits between spaces.

How it fitsA word is one morpheme or several, joined. In a sentence it takes a job.

Who named itLeonard Bloomfield called the word a minimum free form: the smallest form that can be said on its own (Language, 1933).

Ring 6 from the centre

sentence noun

What it isWords put in order to make a claim or ask a question.

How it fitsWords take jobs in a sentence: actor, operator, action, acted-on. The order carries meaning the words alone don’t. The jobs are on Definitions.

syntax noun

What it isThe study of how words are put in order to make sentences.

Who named itFrom Greek syntaxis, an arranging. Apollonius Dyscolus wrote the first Greek book on it, On Syntax, in the second century.

Sources: Britannica, Apollonius Dyscolus.

Ring 7 from the centre

meaning noun

What it isWhat a part, a word or a sentence says, and what it does when someone uses it.

How it fitsMeaning is the outer ring, and every ring inside serves it. Morphemes bring meaning into a word; a sentence brings it into use.

semantics noun

What it isThe study of meaning. The study of meaning in use, in context, is pragmatics.

Who named itMichel Bréal named it, French sémantique, in 1883. Charles Morris (1938) set semantics beside syntax and pragmatics.

Sources: Semantics.

A bridge: phoneme to morpheme

morphophonemics noun

What it isHow a morpheme’s sounds change when it joins other morphemes. Also called morphophonology, or morphonology.

How it fitsIt is the bridge from the morpheme ring back to the phoneme ring. The morpheme stays the same part; its phonemes shift.

Who named itBaudouin de Courtenay studied these alternations in 1894. Nikolai Trubetzkoy proposed morphonology as its own chapter of linguistics in 1929. Leonard Bloomfield’s Menomini Morphophonemics (1939) gave the English name. Noam Chomsky and Morris Halle (1968) argued that English spelling is close to optimal because it keeps morphemes whole. Later studies find that claim goes too far.

Sources: Jan Baudouin de Courtenay; Trubetzkoy’s Orphan (Benjamins); University of Alberta, “Sorry, Chomsky” (2016).

A bridge: phoneme to meaning

orthographic mapping noun

What it isHow a reader stores a word: by connecting its graphemes to its phonemes, so its spelling, pronunciation and meaning are kept together.

How it fitsIt crosses the rings: phonemes, graphemes, morphemes and meaning bonded in one memory. Once a word is mapped, it is read on sight and spelled from memory.

Who named itThe term and the account are Linnea Ehri’s, from her research on how readers learn words by sight. Her 2014 paper sets them out.

Sources: Linnea Ehri, “Orthographic Mapping in the Acquisition of Sight Word Reading, Spelling Memory, and Vocabulary Learning,” Scientific Studies of Reading 18 (2014).

Where the picture comes from

Linguists often draw the levels of language as rings: speech sounds at the centre, meaning in context at the edge. One public-domain version is on Wikimedia Commons, after a figure by the US National Visualization and Analytics Center.

That picture has no graphemes and no orthography. This one adds them, between the sounds and the parts that carry meaning, because spelling is where this site works. The same terms are listed on Definitions.
