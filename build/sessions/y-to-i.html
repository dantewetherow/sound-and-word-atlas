Y to i at the seam

How a spelling would read decides the letter

+ er →

Step 29 of the route: -y, -ly and y turns to i

The words: sadly, slowly, happily, happier, tried, carries. The contrast: try and tried, play and played.

Why does try change and play not?

The rule as it is often taught

Change y to i before a suffix, unless the suffix begins with i.

It works as a mnemonic: it says what to do. It doesn’t say why.

The reason often given, that English words don’t end in i, lacks a premise: once a suffix joins, the y isn’t at the end of anything.

Put the y back,
and read what you’ve written.

The y becomes i where, as y, it would be read wrong in its new place.

happy + er

happyer

Before a vowel, y reads as a consonant, as it does in yes and yellow. A reader says happ·yer.

With i, nothing reads as a consonant: happ·i·er.

The same misread, every time

funny + erfunnyer→ funnier
carry + escarryes→ carries
try + edtryed→ tried

Before a consonant, too

happy + nesshappyness→ happiness
merry + mentmerryment→ merriment
happy + lyhappyly→ happily

Inside a word, a y between consonants invites the reading it has in shyness and dryly. The misreads aren’t certain, but they’re there to be made.

An i lets the sound shorten, as it does when we say these words: happ·i·ness, merr·i·ment.

When the y stays

sly + ly → slyly

Slily would read as slilly: an i between consonants reads short, as in silly.

play + ed → played

The y belongs to a team, ay. It’s read with the a already, so there’s nothing to misread.

carry + ing → carrying

Carriing would set i beside i, a pair English almost never writes. The y keeps the two apart.

The rule, said as reading

The y becomes i at a seam where y would be read wrong. It stays where i would be read wrong, or where it is part of a team.

Learners can test it themselves: write the word both ways, and read each aloud as written.

In the notation

→
  1. The letters, and the bar under pp: two letters, one sound.
  2. A circle for each sound, red for the vowel sounds, the y’s and the i’s among them.
  3. A cup over the short a, and nothing over the y or the i: both are bare, unstressed, with the same sound.
  4. The syllables: happy has two, happier three.
  5. The box around pp stops the reach of the y, and of the i, so the a stays short.
  6. The seams: hap meets the y in happ + y, and the i takes the y’s place in happ + i + er. The letter changes; its job doesn’t.

To take back to class

When a learner writes happyness or carryed, ask them to read it back exactly as written. The misread is the lesson.
